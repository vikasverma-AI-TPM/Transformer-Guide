Foundations — From Neural Nets to Transformers

Foundations — From Neural Nets to Transformers

Why the Transformer showed up, and what it replaced.

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • Transformers (2017, "Attention Is All You Need") drop recurrence entirely and replace it with self-attention — every word attends to every other word in parallel — plus a feed-forward layer, residual connections, and normalization in every block.
  • RNN/LSTM read text sequentially → slow, and prone to losing long-range context (vanishing gradients).
  • Word2Vec embeddings are static — one fixed vector per word, forever.
  • Encoder–decoder + attention (2016) let the decoder "look back" at specific encoder states — the preview idea that became self-attention.
  • Two-stack shape: an encoder reads the input, a decoder generates the output, connected by cross-attention.
  • ULMFiT (2017–18) introduced the pretrain-then-fine-tune recipe that modern LLMs still follow.
WHY

💡 The big picture

Every architecture in this story exists to do one thing: turn language into meaningful numbers, and get better at using context to do it. Plain neural networks could learn patterns but had no sense of order. Word2Vec (a neural network's intermediate output) gave every word a fixed vector — a real leap, but the vector for a word never changed, no matter what sentence it appeared in. Recurrent networks (RNN → LSTM/GRU, 2014–15) finally handled sequences — text read in order — by carrying a hidden state from one word to the next. That unlocked encoder–decoder models for machine translation, then encoder–decoder with attention, which let the decoder "look back" at specific input words instead of relying on one squeezed-down summary.

The catch: RNNs process one token at a time, in order — slow, and prone to losing track of anything said too long ago (the "long‑term dependency" / vanishing‑gradient problem). The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" removed recurrence entirely and replaced it with self‑attention — every word looks at every other word at once — combined with a feed-forward network, residual connections, and normalization in every block. That's the Transformer.

WHAT

🗺️ The full architecture, at a glance

Before going deeper, here's the whole shape you're building toward: two stacks — an encoder that reads the input sentence, and a decoder that generates the output — connected by cross-attention. Every topic after this one fills in one piece of this picture.

Self-Attention — Words Understanding Each Other

Self-Attention — Words Understanding Each Other

"Self-attention = a word looking at other words in the same sentence to understand its meaning."

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • Formula: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKᵀ/√dk)·V.
  • Q = what a word is looking for, K = what a word offers for matching, V = what actually gets passed along once matched.
  • QKᵀ = similarity score between words; dividing by √dk keeps scores stable; softmax turns scores into weights summing to 1.
  • Every word's output is a weighted blend of every word in its sentence → a contextual representation (not "dynamic embedding").
  • Same word, different sentence → different weights → different output vector (classic example: "bank" in a money sentence vs. a river sentence).
  • Self-attention: Q/K/V from the same sequence. Cross-attention: Q from decoder, K/V from encoder. Masked self-attention: Q blocked from future K/V.
WHY

💡 Concept

A Word2Vec-style embedding is static: the word "bank" gets exactly one vector, forever. But "bank" means something different in "money bank grows" than in "river bank flows". Self-attention fixes this by making every word's final representation a weighted blend of every word in its own sentence — so the same starting word can end up at a different point in vector space depending on its neighbors. That's called a contextual representation (some material loosely calls this a "dynamic embedding," but contextual representation/contextual embedding is the standard term worth using in an interview).

WHAT

📖 Worked example (from the notes)

Take the word money in "money bank grows". Self-attention represents it as a weighted sum of every word's vector in the sentence:

money → w₁·money + w₂·bank + w₃·grows

Now compare two sentences that both contain "bank":

S₁: money bank grows
"bank" leans toward money/finance — pulled toward money and grows.
S₂: river bank flows
"bank" leans toward geography — pulled toward river and flows.

Same word, same starting vector — but two different weighted combinations, so two different output vectors. The same logic explains the notes' other example: "I ate the apple while my Apple phone charged" — self-attention pulls one "apple" toward fruit and the other toward company, using surrounding words as evidence.

Straight from the notes
This weighted-sum treatment isn't just for "bank" — every word in the sentence gets rewritten this way. For S₁ = "money bank grow": money → w₁·money + w₂·bank + w₃·grow, bank → w₁·money + w₂·bank + w₃·grow, and grow → w₁·money + w₂·bank + w₃·grow — same three ingredients each time, but the weights (w₁, w₂, w₃) are different for each target word, because each word "looks at" the sentence from its own position.
FORMULA

🧮 Where the weights come from

Those weights aren't guessed — they come from one formula, applied to every word at once:

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( QKᵀ ⁄ √dk ) · V

In plain English: look at how similar every word's Query is to every word's Key, turn those similarity scores into weights that add up to 1, then blend the Values together using those weights.

Q (query), K (key) and V (value) are three projections of the same input embeddings. QKᵀ scores how much every word should attend to every other word; dividing by √dk keeps those scores from getting too large; softmax turns each row of scores into weights that sum to 1 (the w₁, w₂, w₃ above); multiplying by V then builds the weighted sum. Run it on real numbers below.

HOW

🗝️ Q, K, V — a dictionary analogy

The notes borrow a very ordinary idea to make Query/Key/Value click: a Python dictionary. Say D = {a: 1, b: 2, c: 3}. Looking up D[a] means: compare your query ("a") against every key in the dictionary ("a", "b", "c"), and return the matching value (1).

Self-attention does the same thing with vectors instead of exact string matches. Every word produces its own Query, Key, and Value vector. To update "bank," take bank's Query and compare it — via dot product — against the Key of every word in the sentence:

s₁ = Query(bank) · Key(money)  ·  s₂ = Query(bank) · Key(bank)  ·  s₃ = Query(bank) · Key(grow)

A dot product is just "how aligned are two vectors" — e.g. [1,2]·[3,4] = 1×3 + 2×4 = 11, a single similarity score. Feed s₁, s₂, s₃ through softmax to get the weights w₁, w₂, w₃ — then use those weights on each word's Value vector (not its raw embedding) to build the final contextual vector for "bank." Q and K decide how much to listen to each word; V is what gets passed along once you've decided to listen.

Self-attention
Q, K, and V all come from the same sequence — words attending to their own sentence.
Cross-attention
Q comes from the decoder; K and V come from the encoder — the output sentence "reads" the input sentence.
Masked self-attention
Same as self-attention, but a word's Query is blocked from seeing Keys/Values of words that come after it.
Beyond the notes
"Multi-head" attention just means running several of these Q/K/V computations in parallel — each "head" with its own learned projections, free to specialize (one head might track grammar, another might track which pronoun refers to which noun) — then concatenating every head's output back together.

Important nuance
"Full context" isn't unconditional — it depends on which stack you're in. The encoder genuinely sees the whole sequence at once (bidirectional — every position attends to every other position, before and after it). The decoder never gets that: it's masked so each position can only attend to itself and earlier positions (see the masking calculator and the encoder/decoder topics) — otherwise it could "peek" at the answer it's supposed to predict.

The calculator above skips that learned projection for clarity (Q = K = V = the toy embedding) so the softmax‑weighting idea stays front and center.

Inside the Transformer — Encoder & Decoder

Inside the Transformer — Encoder & Decoder

The full "Attention Is All You Need" architecture, step by step.

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • Pipeline: Text → Tokenize → Embed → + Positional Encoding → Self-Attention → Add & Norm → FFN → Add & Norm (× N blocks).
  • Positional Encoding: PE(pos,2i)=sin(pos/10000^(2i/d)), PE(pos,2i+1)=cos(...) — added (not concatenated) to embeddings.
  • Add & Norm = residual connection (x + z) then LayerNorm: LayerNorm(x) = ((x−μ)/√(σ²+ε))·γ+β.
  • Residuals let each layer learn a small improvement over its input instead of a whole new transformation — critical for deep stacking.
  • FFN(x) = ReLU(x·W₁+b₁)·W₂+b₂ — a per-token non-linear transform (512→2048→512 in the original paper).
  • N (block count) is a hyperparameter — small in the original paper, much larger in modern models (exact numbers in the Deep Dive below).
  • Modern decoder-only LLMs (GPT, Llama) drop the encoder entirely and stack only masked self-attention blocks.
WHY

💡 The two stacks

The original Transformer (built for machine translation) has two stacks: an encoder that reads the input sentence, and a decoder that generates the output sentence one token at a time, using both its own previous outputs and the encoder's result. Blocks are structurally identical within each stack — same layer types in the same order, though each has its own independently learned weights, not shared ones. But encoder blocks and decoder blocks are not identical to each other: a decoder block has an extra cross-attention sub-layer (so it can attend to the encoder's output) that an encoder block doesn't have. Later models kept only one side: BERT keeps only the encoder, GPT keeps only the decoder.

WHAT

🔤 Data → output, in one line

Every encoder/decoder block below is really just an unpacking of one pipeline:

Text → Tokenization → Embedding Layer → Positional Encoding → Self-Attention → FFN → Output

One nuance worth pinning down: the embedding layer in a Transformer looks a lot like Word2Vec — it turns each token into a dense vector — but it is not a pretrained Word2Vec model. By default it's a trainable layer that starts from random weights and learns its own word vectors while the rest of the Transformer is training, jointly with everything downstream.

🔀 Why order matters (and why Positional Encoding exists)

Self-attention looks at "every word at once" — which also means, on its own, it has no idea which word came first. But word order changes meaning. Shuffle the sentence below and see for yourself:

That's exactly why Positional Encoding is added to every input embedding: it injects an explicit position signal so the model can tell "Sunny is taking GenAI class" apart from a scrambled bag of the same words — something an RNN got "for free" by reading strictly left-to-right, but a fully parallel Transformer has to be told explicitly.

The classic pair the notes use to make this concrete: "Dog bites man" vs. "Man bites dog" — same three words, opposite meaning, purely from order.

FORMULA

📐 Positional encoding, computed REAL MATH

Definition: Positional encoding is a technique that injects information about a token's position into its embedding, so the model can recover sequence order even though it processes every token in parallel.

A tempting first idea: just tag each token with its index — 1, 2, 3, …, n. The notes flag two real problems with that:

1. No natural limit
Real sentences (or documents) can run to thousands of tokens — there's no fixed ceiling to design around.
2. Discrete, unbounded numbers
Feeding raw integers like 1, 2, 3 … 10000 into a network as a "position vector" gives it wildly different magnitudes to learn from — training becomes unstable.

The fix: encode position with sine and cosine waves instead — smooth, continuous, and always bounded between -1 and +1, no matter how long the sequence gets:

PE(pos, 2i) = sin( pos / 100002i/d_model )
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos( pos / 100002i/d_model )

In plain English: give every position in the sentence its own smooth, bounded wave-based "fingerprint", so the model can tell how far apart two tokens are without ever using a raw, unbounded position number.

pos is the token's position (0, 1, 2, …), d_model is the embedding dimension, and i ranges from 0 up to (d_model/2 − 1) — alternating sine and cosine fills every dimension of the positional vector, one pair at a time. This positional vector is then added (not concatenated) to the word's embedding — same reasoning as Add & Norm below: addition keeps the dimension unchanged.

➕ Add & Norm — residual connections

After self-attention turns inputs x₁, x₂, x₃ into z₁, z₂, z₃, the block doesn't just pass z forward — it adds the original input back in (z + x), then normalizes the result (Layer Normalization), before handing it to the feed-forward layer. That "+x" is the residual (a.k.a. skip connection — the same idea ResNet popularized for CNNs).

Why deep networks need this

Stacked deep networks suffer from vanishing gradients, unstable training, and information getting lost layer over layer.

What the residual buys you

Instead of forcing each layer to learn a "completely new transformation," it only has to learn a small improvement over its input — better gradient flow, faster training, less information loss, and it makes deep stacking (6, 12, 24+ layers) practical at all.

Beyond the notes
This is also why the residual step uses addition (x + z, element-wise, same dimension in and out) rather than concatenation (joining x and z side by side, e.g. [1,2,3] + [5,6,7] → [1,2,3,5,6,7]). Concatenation would grow the vector's size at every layer; addition keeps it fixed, so the same-shaped block can be stacked N times.

The "Norm" half: Layer Normalization

"Add" is the residual step above; "Norm" is what runs on the result right after — Layer Normalization. The notes' own definition: "Layer normalization standardizes the features of each vector to improve training stability and performance" — it normalizes a vector, then scales and shifts it using two learnable parameters.

LayerNorm(x) = ( (x − μ) ⁄ √(σ² + ε) ) · γ + β

In plain English: re-center and re-scale a single vector's own numbers so they sit in a stable, well-behaved range, then let the model learn (via γ and β) whether it wants to stretch or shift that range back out again.

SymbolMeaning
xinput vector (e.g. a token's context embedding)
μmean of that vector's own features
σ²variance of that vector's own features
εa tiny constant, only there to avoid dividing by zero
γlearnable scale parameter (starts at 1)
βlearnable shift parameter (starts at 0) — "gives the model the freedom to choose where the data should be centered"
Worked example, straight from the notes
Input vector x = [0.8, -1.2, 2.4] (a toy context embedding for "money"):
μ = (0.8 + (−1.2) + 2.4) / 3 ≈ 0.67
σ² = [(0.8−0.67)² + (−1.2−0.67)² + (2.4−0.67)²] / 3 ≈ 2.17
√(σ²) ≈ 1.47
x_norm = (x − μ) / 1.47 ≈ [0.09, −1.27, 1.18]
with γ=[1,1,1], β=[0,0,0] → y = x_norm · γ + β = [0.09, −1.27, 1.18] (identity, since γ,β haven't learned anything yet)

Why LayerNorm matters

1. Stabilizes training
Keeps values in a controlled range → prevents exploding or very large activations.
2. Improves gradient flow
Avoids vanishing/exploding gradients → helps deep transformer stacks train smoothly.
3. Faster convergence
Inputs to each layer stay well-conditioned (consistent scale) → the model learns faster.

LayerNorm vs. other scaling methods

MethodFormulaNormalizes across
Min-Max Scaling(x − min) / (max − min)rescales into a fixed range, e.g. [0, 1]
Standard Scaler(x − μ) / σzero mean, unit variance (a column/feature, typically)
Layer Normalization(x − μ) / √(σ² + ε) · γ + βall the features of one vector, then re-scaled/shifted by learnable γ, β

🧠 Why a feed-forward layer at all?

Self-attention's whole job is mixing information across tokens — learning word-level relationships. It never asks "given everything I now know about this token, what should I do with it?" That's the feed-forward network's (FFN) job: it processes each token's vector independently, adding non-linearity so the model can learn genuinely complex patterns, not just straight-line ones.

FFN(x) = ReLU(x·W₁ + b₁)·W₂ + b₂

In plain English: take each token's vector (independently of every other token), pass it through one non-linear "thinking" layer, then project it back down to the original size.

In the original paper this expands each 512-dimensional token vector up to a 2048-dimensional hidden layer and back down to 512 — 512 → 2048 → 512 — giving the network room to learn richer, non-linear structure before compressing back to the block's working size.

🧱 Why stack N encoder/decoder blocks?

🔬 Deep Dive: Why stack N encoder/decoder blocks?▸ Show

The number of blocks (N) is a hyperparameter — an experimental value, not a fixed law. The original paper used 6 encoder blocks and 6 decoder blocks. Blocks within a stack share the same structure; encoder and decoder blocks don't — decoder blocks add an extra cross-attention sub-layer that encoder blocks lack. Stacking helps the model capture deeper relationships, understand longer context, and build hierarchical meaning — each layer refines the representation a bit further:

Layer (illustrative)What it tends to capture
Layer 1Basic word relations
Layer 3Phrase-level meaning
Layer 6Sentence-level understanding
Layer 12Deep context

Modern models push this much further: BERT Base uses 12 layers, BERT Large uses 24, and GPT-family models range from 12 up to 96+ layers.

🔧 How the architecture evolved

🔬 Deep Dive: How the architecture evolved▸ Show

Modern LLMs (GPT, Llama, and almost every other well-known model) do not keep the original encoder–decoder skeleton — they drop the encoder and the cross-attention that connects it to the decoder entirely, and stack only decoder blocks (with masked self-attention) end to end. The original 2017 paper's two-stack, encoder+decoder design was built for translation, where you genuinely have a separate source sentence and target sentence. A general-purpose LLM just keeps predicting the next token of one continuous sequence, so a single masked (decoder-only) stack is all it needs — see the BERT vs. GPT topic for why "decoder-only" became the dominant shape for generative LLMs.

What does carry over from the original architecture into modern decoder-only LLMs is the core machinery this guide covers — masked self-attention, add & norm, feed-forward blocks, stacking N blocks — just refined: RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm, SwiGLU feed-forward layers, grouped multi-query attention with a KV cache for faster inference, and rotary positional embeddings (RoPE) instead of fixed sinusoidal PE.

Beyond the notes
The class notes sketch a Transformer-vs-Llama block diagram without deriving each component — treat the refinements above as a pointer to what to study next, not a full explanation here. The one correction worth internalizing regardless: "Llama-style" does not mean "encoder-decoder with upgrades" — it means decoder-only.

Multi-Head, Cross & Masked Attention

Multi-Head, Cross & Masked Attention

Many viewpoints at once, attending across sequences, and hiding the future.

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • A head = its own WQ, WK, WV. Multi-head = several heads run in parallel, each free to specialize.
  • Formula: MultiHead(Q,K,V) = Concat(head₁,…,head_N)·WO.
  • The original paper used 8 heads — but head count is a tunable hyperparameter, not a law.
  • Masking: add −∞ to future positions before softmax → softmax(−∞)=0 → causal (left-to-right only) attention.
  • Cross-attention: Query from the decoder, Key/Value from the encoder — how the decoder "reads" the source sentence.
WHY

💡 Why one head isn't enough

A head is a self-attention mechanism defined by its own WQ, WK, WV projection matrices. One head means one set of those three matrices — one "viewpoint" on the sentence. Multi-head attention just means running several independent heads in parallel, each with its own learned WQ/WK/WV, so the model gets multiple viewpoints at the same time.

Three reasons this matters:

1. Ambiguous sentences are everywhere in NLP
A single sentence often supports more than one valid reading.
2. A single head can only capture one perspective
One set of Q/K/V learns one kind of relationship well — syntax, or coreference, or position — rarely all of them at once.
3. Real sentences have multiple perspectives simultaneously
Grammar, meaning, and reference all matter at the same time — so the model needs several heads working in parallel, each free to specialize.
The ambiguity examples from the notes
"The chicken is ready to eat." Does that mean the chicken is cooked and ready to be eaten, or that the chicken (some hungry animal) is ready to eat something? One attention pattern might link "chicken" ↔ "ready" as "the thing being prepared"; another might link "chicken" ↔ "eat" as "the one doing the eating." Different heads can hold both relationships at once.

"Man saw the astronomer with the telescope." Who has the telescope — the man, or the astronomer? A single attention pattern picks one attachment; multiple heads can represent both candidate relationships in parallel, leaving later layers (and training) to weigh which one the sentence actually intends.

The original "Attention Is All You Need" paper used 8 heads — but the number of heads (N) is a tunable hyperparameter, not a law of nature. One head → one set of Q, K, V; multi-head → multiple independent sets, computed in parallel and then combined.

FORMULA

🧩 Concat + output projection

🔬 Deep Dive: Concat + output projection▸ Show

Formally:

MultiHead(Q, K, V) = Concat(head₁, …, head_N) · WO
where headi = Attention(Q·WQᵢ, K·WKᵢ, V·WVᵢ)

In plain English: run several independent, smaller attention computations side by side (each with its own learned Q/K/V lens on the sentence), glue their outputs together, then use one more learned matrix to squeeze that wide result back down to the model's normal working size.

Each head runs the full scaled dot-product attention formula independently, on its own smaller projection of Q, K, V (e.g. the original paper splits d_model=512 across 8 heads → 64 dimensions per head). Every head's output is then concatenated side by side into one wide vector, and that wide vector is passed through one more learned matrix, WO (the output projection), which mixes the heads back down to the model's working width — so the next layer still sees a normal-sized vector no matter how many heads ran.

Illustrative 2-head example (toy numbers)
Say each head outputs a 2-dimensional vector for the word "money": head₁ → [0.6, -0.2], head₂ → [0.1, 0.9]. Concatenating gives a 4-dim vector [0.6, -0.2, 0.1, 0.9]. A learned WO (here 4×2, purely illustrative) would then project that back down to a 2-dim output — e.g. something like [0.35, 0.41] — so a 2-head, 2-dim-per-head setup still hands the next layer a plain 2-dim vector, exactly like a single-head setup would.

Masked self-attention is self-attention where future tokens are blocked, so each token can only attend to itself and the tokens before it. Mechanically: add a mask matrix of 0s and −∞ to the scaled score matrix before softmax — 0 for allowed positions (i.e., i ≥ j), −∞ for blocked future positions (i.e., j > i). Since softmax(−∞) = 0, those positions vanish from the weighted sum automatically.

💡 What should I notice?
Notice the upper-right triangle is always exactly 0 when masking is on — that's the causal mask making sure no position can ever see a future token.
What goes wrong without masking, during training
1. Data leakage — the model can see the very tokens it's supposed to be predicting, so it isn't really learning to predict at all.
2. Overfitting / an unrealistic training signal — training accuracy looks great (it's cheating), but that doesn't transfer to inference, where the model genuinely can't see the future — real-world accuracy drops.
WHAT

🔀 Cross-attention

Cross-attention is a mechanism where the Query comes from the decoder, while the Key and Value come from the encoder's output. It's how the decoder "looks at" the source sentence while generating the target — the encoder is run once, and every decoder step re-attends to that same fixed encoder output.

Self-attention
Same sequence checks the importance of a word with respect to other words in that same sequence.
Cross-attention
Checks the importance of a word from one sequence with respect to words in another sequence.
Worked example from the notes: "we are friends" → "हम दोस्त हैं"

English encoder tokens: we, are, friend. Hindi decoder tokens: हम (we), दोस्त (friend), हैं (are). For each Hindi token, its Query is compared against the English Keys (not Hindi keys), and the resulting weights are applied to the English Values:

Contextual(हम)  = w₁·V_we + w₂·V_are + w₃·V_friend
Contextual(दोस्त) = w₁·V_we + w₂·V_are + w₃·V_friend
Contextual(हैं)   = w₁·V_we + w₂·V_are + w₃·V_friend

Since "दोस्त" means "friend," we'd expect its cross-attention weights to land heaviest on V_friend — the decoder token pulling most of its meaning from the matching source word, even though the two sequences are in different languages.

Complete Flow: Trace a Sentence

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • Full order: Encoder (embed → PE → self-attn → add&norm → FFN → add&norm) × N, then Decoder (embed → PE → masked self-attn → add&norm → cross-attn → add&norm → FFN → add&norm) × N, then Linear → Softmax.
  • Training is parallel — the whole target sequence is known and masked at once.
  • Inference is sequential/autoregressive — one token generated at a time, fed back in as input for the next step.
  • The encoder runs once; its output is reused as Key/Value for every decoder step's cross-attention.
  • Final linear layer projects to vocabulary size; softmax turns those scores into a probability distribution over the next token.

BERT vs. GPT — Two Pretraining Philosophies

BERT vs. GPT — Two Pretraining Philosophies

Same architecture family, two different ways of learning from raw text.

⚡ 5-Minute Revision
  • BERT = encoder-only, pretrained with Masked Language Modeling (MLM) — predict a hidden token from context on both sides (bidirectional).
  • GPT = decoder-only, pretrained with autoregressive next-token prediction — predict the next token from everything before it (left-to-right).
  • BERT tends toward understanding/classification tasks; GPT tends toward generation tasks (chat, summarization, translation).
  • Same underlying Transformer block machinery (attention, add&norm, FFN) — the difference is which stack is kept and what the pretraining objective is.
WHY

💡 Concept

BERT (Google, 2018) uses only the encoder and is pretrained with Masked Language Modeling (MLM): hide a random word in a sentence, and train the model to predict it using context from both directions. GPT (OpenAI) uses only the decoder and is pretrained with autoregressive next‑token prediction: given everything so far, predict the next token — the same trick used at inference time to generate text.

GPT-style (next token)
